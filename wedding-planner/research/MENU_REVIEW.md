# Wedding menu review — Mahima Mishra & Mayank Awasthi

**Research date:** 15 October 2026. **Status:** Recommendations for discussion; no menu selections have been changed or approved.

**Read first:** The [Wedding Record](../WEDDING_RECORD.md) remains the canonical menu transcript. This review uses its ten listed meal/snack services, including the undated Reception. “Same service” means the same menu block: actual clock times and simultaneous counter operation are not established. Reception date, city, audiences, guest preferences, caterer recipes, portions and budget remain unknown. Original photographs were not re-transcribed during this review.

## 1. Decision brief

The strongest opportunity is to give each meal a clear character while preserving familiar choices. Current menus provide substantial choice, but repeated counters and rich preparations can reduce the difference guests experience.

Recommended first decisions:

1. **Clarify the 19 January lunch dals.** Dal lapeta is an uncertain transcription; establish whether it is separate from dal fry or the dal served with baati before removing anything.
2. **Differentiate the two large dinners.** Hakka noodles, fried rice, Jini dosa, red/white pasta, Manchow, Blue Lagoon, brownie and mawa bati recur. Allocation is more useful than adding another cuisine.
3. **Review the Sangeet starter group.** Paneer garlic sticks and cheese cigar rolls occupy a similar rich, dairy-led role, especially alongside paneer chilli, Jini dosa and paneer Punjabi.
4. **Simplify Reception desserts by role.** Brownie and waffles overlap as warm bakery desserts; gajar and dry-fruit halwa overlap as rich spoon desserts. Preserve selected contrasts and favourites.
5. **Review 20 January breakfast.** Shahi toast and rasmalai both deliver a rich milk-sweet experience if the toast is prepared as shahi tukda. Choose one unless both are family favourites.
6. **Improve contrast using existing food first.** Plain rice/roti, salad, seasonal fruit, a genuinely different dry vegetable, chutneys and a milder option can contribute more than an extra rich dish.

These are culinary judgements grounded in menu structure and reference recipes, not measured guest preferences or nutritional calculations.

## 2. What the research establishes

| Research finding | Application to this menu | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Food variety involves differences in sensory characteristics; the variety literature includes sensory-specific satiety and increased intake [F01]. | Judge flavour, texture and preparation as well as names. | More intake is not our success measure. These studies do not establish an ideal number of wedding dishes or prove fatigue across three days. |
| Analysis of eight Indian recipe groups found less shared flavour chemistry than randomised comparisons [F02]. | Do not use “more shared molecules = better combination” as a universal menu rule. | Recipe co-occurrence is not a taste trial, a guest survey or evidence that any contrasting buffet dishes will pair well. |
| Pairing research discusses familiarity and perceptual relationships as well as ingredient chemistry [F03]. | Familiar combinations and well-executed contrasts deserve tasting alongside novelty. | Research supplies possible mechanisms, not a guaranteed best pairing. Publisher/university indexed extracts were available; the full review was not retrievable. |
| Primary research found individual differences in liking and consumption of spicy foods [F04]. | Keep a recognisable milder route and allow optional chilli/chutney intensity. | The university abstract was inspected; it does not measure these wedding guests or support age/regional stereotypes. |
| Reference recipes demonstrate ingredient and preparation differences [F05–F25]. | Distinguish real contrast from different names on a shared gravy or sauce. | The caterer's recipe can differ. Recipe-site ratings and “popular” descriptions were not used as representative preference statistics. |

The practical working principle is **familiarity plus meaningful contrast, validated with the family's food**. Repeating an ingredient is not automatically a problem: steamed idli, crisp dosa and a soft uttapam can share a batter family yet feel different.

## 9. People liking: useful evidence and unresolved preferences

Internet research cannot establish what Mahima and Mayank's guests will like. No representative Indian wedding preference dataset or actual guest tasting was obtained. Ingredient popularity, restaurant orders, recipe ratings and social-media enthusiasm are not substitutes for this guest group.

Use preference needs rather than demographic labels:

| Preference need | Existing route | Gap or decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Familiar everyday comfort | Dal, plain rice, roti, dahi, idli | Keep these easy to access and confirm actual spice levels. |
| Rich festive food | Paneer, kofta, dal makhani, sweets | Already well represented; improve distinctions before adding more. |
| Chatpata/crisp food | Pani puri, tikki, palak patta, jhalmuri | Different bases help; repeated sweet-curd toppings may need trimming. |
| Grilled/savoury food | Paneer and pineapple barbecue; tawa vegetables | Existing strengths; consider a non-dairy vegetable preparation. |
| Cheese/pasta/street-food enjoyment | Cheese roll, Jini, pasta, pizza, Chinese | Choose the strongest executions across events, rather than assuming everyone wants all. |
| Soft texture or milder seasoning | Idli, dal-rice, selected soft vegetables | Do not infer needs solely from age. A crunchy chaat-only snack may need a practical alternative for an identified guest. |
| Less-sweet or fresh-tasting choices | Fruit, salad, water, appropriately prepared chaas | Keep seasoning/sugar adjustable; do not label dishes medically suitable. |
| Jain, no onion/garlic, vegan, allergy or other dietary requirement | Not established by the draft names | Resolve separately from taste preferences. Vegetarian does not establish any of these individual requirements. |

The owner's personal paneer preference does not establish the guests' preference. Methi, mustard greens, mustard oil, coriander, very sweet gravies and unusual vegetables can all be enjoyed or disliked; retain agency through choice and actual family knowledge.

### A small tasting that would settle the important decisions

This is an optional practical evaluation, not a new app workflow:

- Compare the caterer's **paneer Punjabi/maharaja/kadhai and kofta gravies side by side**: are the differences recognisable without labels?
- Compare **paneer garlic stick and cheese cigar**, then taste with the proposed surrounding stalls: which adds a distinct experience?
- Try **one complete 19 January lunch plate**, including baati and its dal, before adding another dal or creamy main.
- Compare the **two proposed dessert sets**: brownie/ice cream at Sangeet versus selected traditional sweets at Reception.

Record concrete reasons: too similar, too sweet, pleasing tang, excessive chilli, crispness lost after holding, preferred texture, family association, or difficult service. Blind comparison can help with flavour; named dishes can then be assessed for familiarity and cultural meaning. A few family tasters are informative, not statistically representative. No universal tasting score or invented 8/10 approval is assigned.
